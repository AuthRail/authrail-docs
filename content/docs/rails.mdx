A **Rail** tells AuthRail how to handle an action your application can perform.
A support agent might be allowed to refund a payment only up to its remaining
refundable amount, while another request needs a person's approval. Rails make
those expectations part of the tool call, so the application can act on the
decision before it runs the function or returns its result.

Your team writes and tests the rule in AuthRail, then selects a published
version for the application's **deployment**, the installation whose identity
and rules you manage in AI Inventory. The sidecar evaluates it, and the SDK applies the
answer at the function you connected. The [quickstart](/docs/getting-started)
shows how to connect that first function.

## How the three kinds work together

| Kind | Its responsibility | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Guard Rail | Control tool use, information sent into a call or information returned to the agent. | Remove a private field from a support response. |
| Data Rail | Obtain a business fact that a rule needs. | Read an invoice's status from the account that owns it. |
| Policy Rail | Decide what the organisation permits, based on a published Policy. | Require a named reviewer before creating an estimate. |

These responsibilities stay separate even when one request uses all three.
A Data Rail reads information; it does not pay the invoice. A Guard that finds
no disclosure problem leaves the business permission to the Policy Rail.
The Policy Rail records which Policy Version supplied its authority, so a
later Policy revision needs a deliberate Rail update.

## Create a rule that requires review

Suppose your sales team lets an agent create small estimates, asks a reviewer
to approve larger ones and only permits USD estimates in this application.
This is your team's example policy, rather than an AuthRail-wide financial limit.
Start with a published Policy and a connected estimate-creation function. AuthRail calls the
function's saved description its **Operation Version**: it describes the
arguments, target, business effect and returned information the rules can use.

### 1. Choose the Policy and action

In <a href={AUTHRAIL.clientOrigin}>AuthRail</a>, open **Rails → Create Rail** and choose **Policy Rail**, then the published
**Policy to follow**. Choose **Write rules** or **Suggest with AI**, and select
estimate creation under **Actions registered by your connected applications**.
If it is missing, work with the application's developer through **Connect an
action**, check the proposed fields and effects, and choose **Confirm action**.
This saves the action's description; the developer still connects the real
function and its provider access.

### 2. Describe when approval is needed

For this example, the reviewed action accepts a numeric `amount` and a text
`currency`. The result is an estimate reference. If your application's action
uses different names or represents amounts in minor currency units, use those
reviewed fields and units throughout; do not copy a threshold into a different
amount representation.

The published Policy should contain the passages you intend to implement:

> Estimates must have a positive amount and use USD. Estimates of at most
> 250 USD may be created without further approval. Estimates above 250 USD
> require approval from the named sales reviewer.

Name the Rail **Estimate creation**, choose **Continue to rules**, then use
**Add rule** under **Conditions and outcomes**. Set up these four rules:

| Rule | Field and comparison | Outcome | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unsupported currency | `currency` **Is not** the fixed text value `USD`. | **Deny** | This application creates USD estimates only. |
| Invalid amount | `amount` **At most** the fixed numeric value `0`. | **Deny** | An estimate needs a positive amount. |
| Ordinary estimate | An **All conditions in this group** group: `currency` **Is** `USD`; `amount` **Greater than** `0`; `amount` **At most** `250`. | **Allow** | The estimate is within the team's ordinary authority. |
| Larger estimate | An **All conditions in this group** group: `currency` **Is** `USD`; `amount` **Greater than** `250`. | **Require approval** | A larger estimate needs the sales reviewer's decision. |

For each comparison, choose **A fixed value** under **Compare with**. Enter
numeric amounts as numbers, not quoted text. Cite the corresponding passage
from the selected Policy Version for each rule. The reason is what another
person will use to understand the decision later, so describe the business
expectation rather than repeating an internal rule ID.

If you asked AI to suggest these rules, compare the suggestion with this table
and the cited Policy. The suggestion remains a draft; it cannot decide which
exceptions your organisation intended to allow.

Condition groups let you express alternatives without losing their meaning.
For example, “the invoice is in scope AND (the amount is small OR the customer
is eligible)” differs from three unrelated conditions. Choose **All conditions
in this group** for AND, **Any condition in this group** for OR, and **Must not
match** to negate a condition or group. The fields and values need compatible
types before you can save and test the rule.

When the rule needs information held elsewhere, choose a published Data Rail
for the same action under **Facts for these rules**. When it needs to interpret
text, enable **AuthRail Contextual AI** on the Guard or Policy Rail. The
[Data and Contextual AI guide](/docs/facts) explains how to choose the source,
question and material the assessment may use.

### 3. Try the cases your team will encounter

Under **Examples**, add these inputs and expected outcomes. Use the action's
input fields under `arguments`, rather than entering the whole chat message:

| Example | Amount | Currency | Expected outcome | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary estimate | `80` | `USD` | `allow` | All ordinary-estimate conditions match. |
| Boundary value | `250` | `USD` | `allow` | “At most” includes 250. |
| Needs review | `251` | `USD` | `approval_required` | The larger-estimate rule matches. |
| Unsupported currency | `80` | `EUR` | `deny` | The application is not authorised for that currency. |
| Invalid amount | `0` | `USD` | `deny` | The estimate must be positive. |

For example, the first case uses:

```json
{"arguments":{"amount":80,"currency":"USD"}}
```

Also try omitting `amount`. A missing required value must remain unresolved,
not become zero or count as an ordinary estimate. If you later add a Data Rail,
include missing and stale readings as well. These extra cases help you find a
rule that accidentally permits incomplete requests.

Choose **Save and test examples**, or **Test examples** for a draft you already
saved. Read each expected and actual outcome and the matched rules. If an
example fails, inspect the condition, field type, units and Policy citation
before changing its expected answer. An unmatched set of rules does not imply
permission; AuthRail reports that it could not decide.

Examples evaluate the saved draft without calling the business tool. If you
change it, save and test again. You will check the real integration separately
with safe requests in [Observe](/docs/modes).

### 4. Publish it and choose the reviewers

An authorised person reviews the draft and its examples before publishing.
An assistant can prepare this work, but the person confirms publication.
Choose the published version for the deployment that will use it; publishing
another version later leaves that selection unchanged until you update it.

For an action that can require approval, open **Approval reviewers**. Under
**Who can approve this action?**, select named active members and the required
number of approvals, then **Save reviewers**. One person is enough when that
is the saved requirement. A named reviewer can also be the requester where
permitted, but still has to make the explicit approval decision. The
[approval guide](/docs/approvals) follows the request through review and resumption.

Before connecting a rule to an application, check its
[SDK and sidecar compatibility](/docs/compatibility). SDK 0.1.1 does not include
the automatic Data bindings, ready-made Guard processors or Rail-owned
Contextual AI flow described here. These requirements need an adapter that
implements them; creating the rule in the editor does not add them to an older
installation.

## Select a Guard protection

A Guard draft's **Built-in protections** offer common checks for string fields.
Choose a protection, the field and whether to apply it before the call or
before returning the result. **Supported formats and limits** explains what
the selection can recognise or change.

| Protection | What it does | Where its coverage ends |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recognised credentials | Block a matching field, or replace its entire string with `[REDACTED]`. | Recognises four PEM private-key headers, fixed-length GitHub tokens and AWS access-key IDs. Other, encoded or split secrets may be missed. |
| Email addresses | Block or remove a field containing a common ASCII email address. | Does not cover every international or obfuscated address or every category of personal information. |
| Payment-card number formats | Block or remove a field containing a 13–19 digit Luhn-valid format, with supported spaces or hyphens. | A matching number is not necessarily an active card and may be another identifier. |
| Always withhold a selected field | Replace the selected string with `[WITHHELD]`, without relying on a detector. | Changes that field only. |
| Approved destinations | Compare a call's destination with a list of allowed strings. | Does not normalise URLs, inspect DNS, validate redirects or isolate a network. |
| Attempts to bypass instructions | Ask Contextual AI whether selected text tries to bypass instructions, approval or access restrictions. | Requires permission to send that material for assessment; uncertainty cannot clear the check. |
| Sensitive disclosure | Ask Contextual AI about authentication secrets, private financial account details or explicitly confidential text. | Covers the selected material and question, not every sensitive category. |

### Work through a result-field protection

Consider a support lookup that returns `summary` and `internalNote`, both text
fields in its reviewed result. The agent needs `summary`, while `internalNote`
should never appear in its reply. A detector is unnecessary when the entire
field is private: choose **Always withhold a selected field**, select **Result · Internal
note** under **Checked field**, then choose **Add protection**. The description
in your editor comes from your own reviewed field, so it may use different
capitalisation.

Under **Examples**, set **Boundary** to **After the tool result** and
**Expected outcome** to **Cleared**. If you use the JSON editor, put the returned
fields inside `result`, so they match the reviewed result field paths:

```json
{"result":{"summary":"The customer's access has been restored.","internalNote":"Internal escalation notes for the support team."}}
```

The draft example runs the built-in transform before checking the Guard, so
Cleared means its required withholding was satisfied. It does not mean the
original private field may be released.

With a compatible integration in Enforce, the result released by the SDK is:

```json
{"summary":"The customer's access has been restored.","internalNote":"[WITHHELD]"}
```

Check that the application's reply uses that checked result. Returning the
original object through a second channel would defeat the field protection.
The support lookup's Policy Rail still decides whether the caller may perform
the lookup; withholding a field does not grant that business permission.

Test a missing field as well as an ordinary result and inspect the reported
reason. A missing required target or an unavailable processor must leave the
protection unsatisfied. In Observe, compare the advisory result decision while
remembering that the application still receives its unchanged business result.
The always-withhold built-in needs a compatible integration; installing SDK
0.1.1 alone does not provide that processor.

For removal, the whole selected string changes, rather than only the matched
characters. The application must use the arguments the SDK gives its function
and return the checked result. Similarly, a destination rule helps only when
the function uses that destination and prevents redirects to an unchecked one.
If a required processor is missing or fails, Enforce holds the call or result
instead of releasing the original field.

Enforce can stop a call or withhold its result. Observe leaves the business
input and result unchanged while showing what the rules would decide. Material
sent for contextual assessment still goes through the required local selection
and filtering first, in either mode. A later model answer cannot undo disclosure.

<summary>Supported local detection formats</summary>

The credential profile recognises the PEM opening markers `PRIVATE KEY`,
`RSA PRIVATE KEY`, `EC PRIVATE KEY` and `OPENSSH PRIVATE KEY` in the standard
`-----BEGIN …-----` form. It also recognises `ghp_`, `gho_`, `ghu_`, `ghs_` and
`ghr_` followed by exactly 36 ASCII alphanumeric characters; `github_pat_`
followed by exactly 82 ASCII alphanumeric or underscore characters; and `AKIA`
or `ASIA` followed by exactly 16 uppercase ASCII letters or digits. A token
embedded in a longer alphanumeric or underscore token does not match.

The email profile accepts an ASCII local part of up to 64 characters and a
complete address of up to 254 characters, with a final alphabetic domain suffix
of 2–63 characters. Consecutive dots and a trailing dot in the local part do
not match. The check recognises a format; it does not validate a mailbox.

The card profile removes ASCII spaces and hyphens from a numeric run, checks
for 13–19 digits, rejects a run made of one repeated digit and checks the Luhn
checksum. Always-withhold uses no detection profile and replaces the chosen
string unconditionally in Enforce.

These checks read the selected string fields. They do not automatically inspect
attachments, images, encoded content or every nested field. Include examples
that should and should not match your actual data. Each published version fixes
its behaviour, so adopting another profile remains a versioned change.

</details>

## Understand how rules combine

A denial takes priority over an allow, and required approval takes priority over
an ordinary allow. Other compatible requirements, such as obtaining a fact or
processing a field, apply together. When a necessary value is missing or two
requirements conflict, AuthRail reports that problem rather than granting
permission. The [Action](/docs/records) explains which versions and reasons
contributed to a particular decision.

For financial examples, choose the fact that matches the operation. Payouts
must fit freshly verified available funds in the requested currency; refunds
must fit the selected payment's remaining refundable amount. Neither example
introduces a universal fixed amount ceiling.

<details className="guide-details">
<summary>Application-specific processing and isolation</summary>

A custom handler is application code for a processing step the built-ins do not
provide. Its developer registers the handler's ID, kind and code fingerprint in
the SDK, and the Guard names the matching version and field. The adapter runs
it and signs evidence linking the processing to the content used by the rule.
The handler must be deterministic and free of business effects: it should not
send a message or move money while processing text.

A missing handler, conflicting target or mismatched output leaves the Guard
unsatisfied. Its signed report identifies the application's assertion, without
independently establishing how good its filtering is. Replacing a literal demo
marker, for example, demonstrates the interface rather than a secret detector.

Text processing also cannot establish filesystem or network isolation. Where
that is needed, use a real containment mechanism such as the optional
[OpenShell integration](/docs/openshell), alongside the
[execution-access review](/docs/security).

</details>
